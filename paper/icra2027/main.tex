\begin{abstract}
Action-chunking robot policies predict multiple future actions from each
observation.  Their execution horizon determines how long this prediction is
followed before the policy observes and replans.  Recent adaptive-execution work
primarily chooses one prefix or boundary for the chunk as a whole; even audited
methods with component-aware signals aggregate them into one synchronized
decision.  We study whether physically meaningful action groups must accept new
predictions on the same schedule, and whether their temporal reliability varies
within an episode.  Our fixed group-specific executor leaves the base policy
frozen: an expired group accepts its part of a fresh full chunk, while
non-expired groups retain their earlier predicted sequences.  On a frozen ACT
policy in LIBERO Object, a complete $5\!\times\!5$ arm/gripper landscape over 50
official states yields 47/50 successes for $(h_a,h_g)=(4,16)$, versus 42/50 for
budget-matched global $h=4$ at approximately 0.252 policy queries per step.  An
offline, teacher-forced audit over 454 demonstration episodes further finds
group-dependent prediction-error structure and phase-conditioned error profiles,
including early--late differences for arm translation and gripper error.
Across the remaining nine tasks, off-diagonal configurations are repeatedly
competitive, including six no-higher-query-rate improvements over global points.
We formulate, but do not implement or evaluate, group-wise temporal reliability
estimation as a route to horizons $h_g(t)$.  No adaptive-execution performance
claim is made.
\end{abstract}
