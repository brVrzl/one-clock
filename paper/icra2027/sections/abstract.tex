Action-chunked policies are commonly summarized by one temporal execution
parameter for the whole action, which can hide distinct temporal sensitivity
across action groups; we instead measure same-target execution with
component-resolved source assignments. In a frozen $140$-block cross-suite ACT
confirmation, assigning the old same-target relationship to the gripper rather
than the arm changed success by $\ConfirmFOReverseDelta$ percentage points
(paired 95\% CI $\ConfirmFOReversePairedCI$~pp; task-cluster 95\% CI
$\ConfirmFOReverseClusterCI$~pp), with positive leave-one-task-out effects for
all ten tasks; a post-hoc factorial interaction was
$\PosthocInteractionDelta$~pp, and both its paired
$\PosthocInteractionPairedCI$~pp and task-cluster
$\PosthocInteractionClusterCI$~pp intervals excluded zero. Across Object
development and frozen cross-suite confirmation, arm-only h16 commitment
produced no aggregate gain over fully reactive execution, whereas coherent h16
execution was reliably above the arm-only decomposition in both cohorts; the
significant development-set C2 penalty itself did not replicate. Although
\textsc{hard-h16} had the highest aggregate ACT success where evaluated, its
advantage over \textsc{FO20} was not statistically reliable in either relevant
ACT cohort; the result therefore supports component-resolved temporal
measurement rather than a universal executor recommendation.
