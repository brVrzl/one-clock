\section{Limitations and Open Questions}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evidence is simulation-only and limited to the related pick-and-place tasks
of LIBERO Object, one frozen ACT checkpoint, and one partition: six
arm/end-effector dimensions versus a one-dimensional gripper.  It does not
establish cross-suite, cross-policy, cross-embodiment, dexterous-hand, or
real-robot generalization.  RoboTwin, RoboDojo, force sensing, and physical-robot
evaluation are pending directions rather than reported experiments.

All evaluated horizons are static.  Per-task best configurations are
retrospective oracle-style analyses and cannot be deployed without a selection
rule.  The universal static $(4,16)$ pair is a strong baseline, but current
experiments do not establish a within-episode dynamic benefit: the common-set
per-task group oracle reaches macro success 0.779 versus 0.734 for universal
$(4,16)$, leaving a 0.045 task-dependent static oracle gap.  Moreover, $(4,16)$
uses substantially more policy queries than low-query global $h=16$ (0.252
versus 0.065 queries per environment step).  These observations motivate
testing whether online adaptation can use short horizons selectively rather than
throughout an episode.  A next-stage study must separate task-level static
selection from genuine phase-dependent gain, define an online observable signal,
and compare at controlled query budgets.

The persistence audits establish heterogeneous and phase-conditioned prediction
error structure, not the performance of a reliability estimator.  Gate-1 is a
teacher-forced comparison with demonstrated future actions, and Gate-2A uses
right-censored targets with a common 38-step analysis window; neither contains
closed-loop success labels.  We therefore do not claim that the proposed
$R_g(k)$ estimator is calibrated, that Eq.~\ref{eq:adaptive_horizon} improves
execution, or that any online dynamic horizon rule works.  Training a
group-wise estimator and evaluating dynamic execution remain future validation.

Finally, group-specific execution deliberately composes actions from different
source chunks.  The retained components were jointly predicted with components
that may no longer be executed, so temporal inconsistency can arise across
groups.  We have not established why particular horizon assignments succeed.
Exploratory trace differences in action variation and sign changes are
descriptive, not causal.  Future work should test synchronization rules and
cross-chunk consistency controls before learning a group-specific scheduler.
