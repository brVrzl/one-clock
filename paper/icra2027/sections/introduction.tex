\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Action-chunked robot policies predict a sequence of future controls from each
observation rather than a single immediate action~\cite{zhao2023act}. This
interface creates a temporal execution choice even when policy weights remain
fixed. At physical time $t$, multiple stored predictions can refer to that same
target time: a recent query contributes an early chunk offset, whereas an older
query contributes a later offset. An executor must decide which eligible
prediction to apply and when to request another chunk. Execution is therefore
not only a question of how much computation to amortize. It also determines
which temporal relationship between observation, prediction, and target time
enters the closed loop.

Most analyses and execution mechanisms summarize this choice for the action as
a whole. They study one execution horizon, one source-age relationship, or one
acceptance, aggregation, or replanning decision for the complete predicted
chunk~\cite{lazzati2026actionchunking,wang2026autohorizon,chen2026a3}. Such
summaries are useful, but they can hide structure when the action vector joins
controls with different physical roles. In our LIBERO action
contract~\cite{liu2023libero}, six dimensions command relative end-effector arm
motion and one dimension commands the gripper. The two groups act through
different mechanisms and need not have the same behavioral sensitivity to a
shared temporal relationship. Assigning one scalar temporal descriptor to all
seven dimensions can therefore erase a factor that matters during execution.
This matters for interpretation as well as aggregate performance. A
whole-action contrast cannot show whether its effect is shared across
components, concentrated in one group, or varies materially by action group.
Conversely, similar task success under two whole-action settings does
not establish that their components are temporally insensitive. Without
component-resolved assignments, these possibilities remain aliased.

We study this issue as a measurement question using controlled execution
interventions, not as a new adaptive executor. All interventions use frozen
trained policies and alter only which same-target predictions supply the arm
and gripper at execution time. Policy checkpoint, observation pipeline, task
configuration, and paired initial states remain matched across conditions.
Same-target validity is central because source age and prediction offset change
together when both identify a prediction for the current physical time. Our
estimand is therefore the combined closed-loop effect of that temporal
relationship for each action group, not a pure effect of observation age. The
design asks whether one relationship has different behavioral consequences
when assigned to different components. Our primary scope is simulation in
LIBERO, using the official LeRobot ACT implementation~\cite{cadene2024lerobot}
with frozen checkpoints, relative end-effector control, and no temporal
ensemble or action smoothing.

The frozen cross-suite factorial provides the clearest evidence for component
dependence. FO20 exceeded Reverse20 by $\ConfirmFOReverseDelta$ percentage
points, with $\ConfirmFOReverseDiscordance$ discordance and positive aggregate
effects in $\ConfirmFOReverseLoto$ leave-one-task-out analyses. Yet FO20
exceeded Fresh by only $\ConfirmFOFreshDelta$ percentage points, and both
intervals for that contrast included zero. Post-hoc factorial decomposition
also yielded a positive statistical interaction, indicating that the temporal
effect associated with one action group depended on the relationship assigned
to the other. These results support a component-resolved measurement claim,
not a general recommendation to deploy FO20.

The fixed-h16 decomposition narrows the executor interpretation across two
cohorts. In Object development, the arm-only C2 executor was below Fresh, but
this penalty did not reproduce on frozen cross-suite confirmation. In contrast,
coherent \textsc{hard-h16} execution remained above C2 in both cohorts. Arm-only
commitment therefore does not reproduce coherent fixed-h16 performance. This
bounded comparison does not identify a gripper causal contribution because C2
and \textsc{hard-h16} differ in their complete execution relationship and
query schedule.

This work makes three contributions. First, it defines a same-target,
component-resolved measurement and intervention design that makes source-age
and chunk-offset coupling explicit. Second, it establishes frozen cross-suite
factorial evidence that temporal assignment depends strongly on action-group
identity. This evidence combines the primary FO20--Reverse20 contrast with a
clearly labeled post-hoc statistical interaction. Third, it gives a
cross-cohort fixed-h16 decomposition and bounded scope evidence. Arm-only
commitment does not reproduce coherent h16 execution in either cohort, while
the Object C2-under-Fresh penalty itself does not replicate. Spatial and one
heterogeneous SmolVLA pilot~\cite{shukor2025smolvla} provide negative and mixed
scope evidence rather than replication across policy classes. Together, these
measurements support a narrow thesis: whole-action temporal parameters can
conceal behaviorally important component-dependent structure in
action-chunked robot policies.
